Park History

Sourced

At Harpers Ferry the Potomac River cuts through the Blue Ridge and takes in the Shenandoah, leaving a V-shaped water gap that Thomas Jefferson called perhaps one of the most stupendous scenes in nature, worth a voyage across the Atlantic. That gap made the place a crossroads from the earliest human habitation onward: American Indians first, then European settlers, and in 1747 Robert Harper’s ferry across the Potomac. Congress authorized a federal armory here on June 15, 1796; when production began in 1801 it was the nation’s second, and in its workshops new ideas about interchangeable parts were tried and tested while large-scale manufacturing turned a quiet rural village into one of the leading industrial centers of its day. [1] [2]

In October 1859 John Brown and his followers seized the armory, determined to arm enslaved people and spark a rebellion; the raid failed, but his trial and execution, in the park brochure’s words, propelled the nation toward civil war. Harpers Ferry then changed hands eight times, and in September 1862 Stonewall Jackson captured nearly 12,700 Union soldiers here — the largest surrender of U.S. troops in the war. After it, Storer College opened on Camp Hill in 1867 as a school primarily for formerly enslaved people, and in 1906 hosted the second conference of the Niagara Movement, where W. E. B. Du Bois demanded equality and civil rights. Congress authorized Harpers Ferry National Monument in 1944; it became a national historical park in 1963 and now covers 3,745 acres in West Virginia, Maryland and Virginia. [1] [2]

Indigenous peoples

People first lived in the Shenandoah Valley in the Paleoindian period, roughly 11,000 to 9,600 B.C. A 1989–1990 excavation in Lower Town recovered 1,585 artifacts — ceramics, stone tools, postholes and hearths — from Late Archaic and Early Woodland occupations around 1200 to 500 B.C. By the time Europeans arrived about 1600, the valley had held no permanent villages for nearly a century; Tuscarora and Shawnee people lived there in temporary settlements, and Iroquois raiding parties used it as a route to attack southern nations such as the Catawba and Cherokee. A series of treaties pushed Native nations west; in the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster the Iroquois sold their land claims to the Virginia Colony and moved west of the Allegheny Mountains. The 2017 ethnographic overview could identify no Native American groups with ties to the park’s resources, quotes an earlier park study that there are no federally recognized tribes in the vicinity, and acknowledges that the park has generally devoted more of its resources to the town’s historic remains than to its pre-contact past. [3]

Black history

During and after the war, people escaping slavery, called “contrabands,” poured into Harpers Ferry; by the mid-to-late 1860s about 700 African Americans lived in town, double the prewar number. Freewill Baptists opened a school for refugees in the armory paymaster’s Lockwood House in 1864, and on October 2, 1867, Storer College began classes there with nineteen students. Students were bullied by local residents and at times had to be escorted into town by hired militiamen. For 25 years Storer was the only institution in West Virginia offering African Americans secondary and post-secondary education; Frederick Douglass was among its first trustees. [3] [2]

In August 1906 the Niagara Movement held its second conference at Storer, and participants walked from the college to John Brown’s Fort, then standing on the Murphy Farm, going barefoot as they approached it to commemorate Brown; the conventions led to the founding of the NAACP in 1909. Storer closed in 1955, after the State of West Virginia withdrew its support. [3] [2]

Slavery

Harpers Ferry sat in Jefferson County, Virginia, which in the first half of the nineteenth century held twice as many enslaved people as any other county in present-day West Virginia: 4,157 in 1840, 4,341 in 1850, and 27 percent of the population by 1860. In Harpers Ferry and Bolivar most owners held one or two people. Newspaper accounts cited in the 2017 ethnographic overview show people bought and sold frequently; an 1857 notice reported free Black people sold by court order in front of the Charles Town courthouse for remaining in the state contrary to law; and escapes toward Pennsylvania were frequent enough that the Virginia Slave Insurance Company was formed in Charles Town to insure against them. [3]

On October 16, 1859, Brown led 21 men against an arsenal holding 100,000 arms. The first man killed was Heyward Shepherd, an African American baggage handler for the B&O Railroad. By noon on October 17, the park’s own 2006 armory study records, drunken vigilantes encircled the armory and several captured raiders were brutally executed; U.S. Marines took Brown at daybreak on October 18. [3] [4]

Labor & the New Deal

The armory began in 1801 with 28 armorers who turned out 293 flintlock muskets that year; by 1810 it employed almost 200 workers in 28 buildings. Its earliest workforce was mostly highly skilled gunsmiths using hand tools, each able to make an entire gunstock or forge and assemble a whole musket lock, and the Ordnance Department’s drive to impose factory discipline met resistance. When Superintendent Thomas Dunn posted strict rules and inspections that cut piece-work wages, a disgruntled former worker Dunn had refused to rehire, Ebenezer Cox, murdered him in his office in early 1830; Cox’s execution, the park’s 2006 study says, made him a folk hero to the armorers. In 1842, after Major Henry Craig imposed a fixed workday enforced by a clock, the entire workforce struck for a week and sent a delegation to President John Tyler protesting that they were being made into “mere machines of labor.” The protest failed: Congress passed, and Tyler signed, a law placing military superintendents over all U.S. armories and arsenals. [4]

REVISE: Publications (6 items)

Publications (6 items) 0 photos

The story of Harpers Ferry NHP is inseparable from the lives of the people who were enslaved here. For generations, their stories went untold. The interpretive materials now targeted for revision were created to ensure these voices would finally be heard.

Park staff flagged handouts saying townspeople used the body of a dead raider for “target practice” and that “a drunken mob murders” raider William Thompson, and proposed softer wording: “They threw his body into the river and continued to fire shots at it,” and “Some townspeople, enraged by the mayor’s death, kill William Thompson and toss his body into the Potomac.” A Civil War driving tour that quotes secession documents on the cause of secession was also flagged. [NPS review record]

Why this matters: The history of slavery at Harpers Ferry NHP was suppressed for generations. The materials now being targeted represent decades of work by historians, descendants of enslaved people, and community advocates to ensure these stories are finally told. Removing them would re-erase history that has only recently been acknowledged.
