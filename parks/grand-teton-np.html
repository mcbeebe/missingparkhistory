Park History

Sourced

With no foothills in the way, the jagged peaks and deep canyons of the Teton Range rise straight out of the valley of Jackson Hole. The rocks at the range’s core, 2.7 billion years old, are among the oldest in North America, yet the mountains are among the youngest in the world: for about 10 million years earthquakes on the Teton fault have lifted the range while the valley floor dropped, until the same rock layers lie nearly 30,000 feet apart. Glaciers up to 3,500 feet thick flowed south from Yellowstone and filled the valley, while alpine glaciers carved the jagged skyline and left moraines that now dam lakes like Jenny Lake. The 310,000-acre park shelters some 1,200 kinds of plants, 300 of birds and more than 60 of mammals. [1]

People have used this valley for roughly 11,000 years, coming seasonally to hunt and gather because the winters were too severe to stay; permanent residents, farmers and ranchers, came only from the mid-1880s. [2] [1] Protecting it took half a century of fighting. Congress created a 96,000-acre park around the peaks and lakes on February 26, 1929. John D. Rockefeller Jr., through the Snake River Land Company, bought more than 35,000 acres of the valley floor, and in 1943 President Franklin Roosevelt proclaimed Jackson Hole National Monument — over strong local opposition, including a heavily armed cattle drive across the monument joined by the actor Wallace Beery. A compromise merged park and monument on September 14, 1950, after Rockefeller gave 32,117 acres to the nation; the HRS calls the result the culmination of 50 years of work, often marred by bitter controversy. [3]

Indigenous peoples

Jackson Hole’s winters were too severe for year-round living, so for millennia its peoples came in season, following ripening plants and migrating game; the oldest buried cultural deposits in the park date to about 5,850 years ago. The mountain-dwelling people the first trappers met were Eastern Shoshone — the Tukudika, or Sheep-Eaters, who were still without horses when they were placed on the Wind River Reservation around 1868. The Shoshone still keep close ties here: in their belief system high peaks are “Power Mountain,” places to enter the spirit world through visions. [2]

Lt. Gustavus Doane, who led a near-disastrous winter expedition into Jackson Hole in 1876, is part of that history too. [4] In June 2022 Yellowstone’s Mount Doane was renamed First Peoples Mountain; the Yellowstone National Park news release announcing the change states that in 1870 Doane led an attack on a band of Piegan Blackfeet, the Marias Massacre, in which at least 173 people were killed, many of them women, elders and children sick with smallpox, and that he bragged of it for the rest of his life. [5] The Blackfeet are among the tribes Grand Teton lists as traditionally associated with the park. [6]

Climate & environment

Only remnant glaciers remain in the Tetons, and they are shrinking. The park’s 2017 foundation document reports that its glaciers and snowfields are getting smaller each year; records show both advance and retreat, but the overall trend is retreat, and models of Wyoming’s alpine glaciers under projected climate change predict the near-complete disappearance of Teton Glacier within 30 to 75 years. A 1968 USGS map showed 9 named glaciers and about 136 smaller glaciers or perennial snowfields, but the park has never completed a thorough inventory. The same document lists glacier retreat, thinner winter snowpack and lower lake levels among the effects of climate change on the park’s scenery, and notes that landslides and avalanches are exacerbated by increasing climate variability, including more extreme events. [6] The brochure singles out whitebark pine, a keystone food source for grizzly bears and red squirrels, as sensitive to global climate change. [1]

Labor & the New Deal

When the Bureau of Reclamation dammed Jackson Lake, the shoreline trees were flooded and killed, and dead and downed trees ringed the lake. Clearing them was one of the Civilian Conservation Corps’ biggest undertakings in the valley. Young men aged 17 to 25 were stationed in camps at Leigh Lake, Lizard Point and near Colter Bay, and from 1934 at Camp NP-4 at the south end of Jenny Lake, manned through 1942. Between 1933 and 1937 they cleared the bulk of 7,000 acres of shoreline; by September 1936 crews were finishing the last 85 acres. They also built the Jenny Lake campground, trails to Phelps Lake and Garnet Canyon, telephone lines and staff housing, and several buildings from the Jenny Lake camp, meant to be temporary, were moved elsewhere and, the HRS reports, remain in use. [3]

In 1872, the Grand Tetons' dramatic peaks rising 13,000 feet from the valley floor captured the imagination of conservationists who eventually protected them as Grand Teton National Park in 1929.

Grand Teton Mountain reaches 13,775 feet and dominates the Teton Range, which rises abruptly from the floor of Jackson Hole. The park was created after fierce local opposition; Jackson Hole National Monument was created in 1943 and later merged into the park. Contains pristine glacial lakes, abundant wildlife, and some of the most dramatic mountain scenery in North America.

From the internal review records: “In January 1870, Doane participated in what is now known as the Marias Massacre, at which, the U.S. Army killed over 170 Piegan Blackfeet, including many women, elders, and children. Doane wrote fondly about this attack and bragged about it for the rest of his life. "”

Why this matters: Grand Tetons showcase the raw power of mountain geology—where uplift and erosion created one of Earth's most dramatic landscapes. Over 3.3 million people visit this site each year.
