Park History

Sourced

Cumberland Island is the largest and southernmost of Georgia’s barrier islands: 17.5 miles long, up to 3 miles wide and 36,415 acres, nearly half of it salt marsh, mudflat and tidal creek. Live oak forest draped in Spanish moss gives way to interdune meadows, freshwater ponds where alligators bellow in spring, rear dunes up to 45 feet high at the north end and almost 18 miles of beach, where loggerhead sea turtles nest in the most significant numbers on the Georgia coast. The ocean is the dominant force here, constantly building and eroding the shore. [1] [2]

People have used the island for more than 4,000 years, and the brochure lists what they left: shell middens, Spanish pot shards from the mid-1500s, a standing row of slave cabin chimneys, and the ruins of mansions. Revolutionary War general Nathanael Greene bought land here in 1783; his widow Catherine built the four-story tabby house called Dungeness; and from 1881 Thomas Carnegie and his wife Lucy acquired most of the island for winter estates. Carnegie family members, government officials, conservation groups and the Park Service worked together to create the national seashore on October 23, 1972, and Congress designated wilderness on the island in 1984, now the largest designated wilderness on any East Coast barrier island. [1] [2]

Indigenous peoples

Shell middens across the island are, in the 2014 foundation document’s words, the most conspicuous clues to a complex American Indian population that once prospered here. Indians knew the island by names including Tacatacuru, according to the 1977 historic resource study, whose author calls its people Timucuans. In 1587 the Franciscan Baltasar López founded the mission of San Pedro de Mocama, probably at the south end (Mocama meant “on the sea” in Timucua), and a second mission, San Pedro y San Pablo de Porturiba, followed in the north, probably after 1595. In 1602 López counted 792 Christian Indians on the island. [2] [3]

The missions did not last. As the English pushed south from Carolina, Creek allies of the English struck missions as far south as Jekyll Island in 1680; amid revolts and an exodus of Indians to the English, Spain’s governor ordered all missions north of Amelia Island removed in 1686, and those on Cumberland were abandoned. [3]

Black history

Union forces occupied Cumberland Island on March 2, 1862. Island tradition, recorded in a 1978 National Register nomination, holds that freedpeople driven off the Stafford, Rayfield and Dungeness plantations settled at the north end as squatters. In 1890 Mason Burbank, part-owner of the High Point hotel, bought five acres at Half Moon Bluff, divided them into 42 lots, and sold them to Black residents, some of them former slaves or their descendants; the nomination suggests the hotel owners wanted assurance of long-term employees. A log building serving as church and school went up in 1893, and the present First African Baptist Church was built in 1937. The foundation document describes the Settlement as home to several African American families who staffed the island’s hotels and estates. [3] [4] [2]

Slavery

By the late 1700s, the foundation document says, a plantation system built on enslaved Africans had taken root on Cumberland. After Nathanael Greene’s death in 1789, Catherine Greene and her second husband Phineas Miller sold live oak timber for the first ships of the U.S. Navy, and sea island cotton became the island’s most profitable crop. The number of enslaved Africans rose from about 200 in 1835 to 455 in 1850 as cotton production grew, while the white population probably never exceeded 60; by 1860 the island had about 13 plantations. [1] [2]

At Robert Stafford’s plantation, famous for sea island cotton, the slave quarters stood about a kilometer east of the main house. A 2003 Park Service report counts the remains of 26 chimneys of tabby and brick there, 19 still standing. After the Civil War, according to writers the 1977 study cites, Stafford burned the quarters and sent away the freedpeople who had returned. [5] [3]

Climate & environment

The 2014 foundation document expects climate change to bring more frequent and intense storms and rising seas, likely causing more shoreline erosion and the loss of archeological resources, and projects significant effects on coastal areas from inundation and higher temperatures. A 2004 U.S. Geological Survey assessment of nearly 30 kilometers of the island’s shoreline rated 22 percent at very high vulnerability to sea-level rise and 28 percent at high vulnerability; the nearby Fernandina Beach tide gauge had recorded relative sea-level rise of about 2 millimeters a year over 103 years. [2] [6]
