Indigenous & Native History

Park History

Sourced

Tlingit people had thrived for countless generations on the island they called Shee, in a land of forest, rivers and sea where a warm current keeps Sitka’s harbor free of ice all year. Salmon came in staggering numbers on the spawning runs; from cedar and other conifers of the rain forest they built multi-family plank houses and carved canoes up to 60 feet long. At the mouth of the Indian River, where the park now lies, the river, the ocean and the coastal rain forest meet in a habitat rich enough that pink salmon runs sometimes pack the river with more than 1.3 million fish. [1]

Russian fur traders, called Anooshi by the Tlingit, pushed into Southeast Alaska under Alexander Baranov of the Russian-American Company. In 1802 Tlingits, especially the Kiks.ádi clan of Shee At’iká, attacked his new outpost north of Sitka and killed most of its Russian and Aleut inhabitants. In 1804 Baranov returned with Russians, Aleuts and the warship Neva; the Kiks.ádi, led by their war chief K’alyaan, held a fort at the mouth of the Indian River, repulsed a landing and wounded Baranov, then withdrew after a siege when their gunpowder ran out. Russia made Sitka the company’s headquarters, and in 1867 sold Alaska to the United States. [1]

In 1890 the federal government reserved the Indian River site as a public park; it became Sitka National Monument in 1910, and in 1972 Public Law 92-510 enlarged it, added the Russian Bishop’s House and made it Sitka National Historical Park. The 2012 foundation document calls it the site of a major armed resistance of Alaska Native people to Russian colonization. [1] [2]

Indigenous peoples

Thornton’s 1998 study for the Park Service describes the Indian River as Kiks.ádi territory: the clan possessed it and kept seasonal subsistence camps on the lower river until the late 1800s. Its 1804 fort, Shís’ḵ’i Noow (best translated “Green Wood Fort”), stood on a new kind of fort site, designed to defend against large Russian ships and their weaponry; shallow tidelands kept keeled vessels off. From the Kiks.ádi perspective, Thornton writes, the battle was an unwarranted attack on their sovereignty; memorials for the dead began perhaps as early as 1806, and the withdrawal is now retraced as the Kiks.ádi Survival March. The 1910 proclamation listed the graves of Russians killed in the battle but not Tlingit ones. The Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded in Sitka in 1912, proposed the Southeast Alaska Indian Cultural Center, opened in the park in 1969. Thornton concludes that Tlingit social and symbolic ties to the park remain strong, while subsistence ties have been largely severed. [3]

Women

According to Herb Hope, as Thornton reports, women gathered kelp to make the green palisade logs slippery. Oral history handed down to Herman Kitka credits a woman interpreter, apparently married to a Russian officer, with warning Katlian (the brochure’s K’alyaan) that the Castle Hill fort would not stand up to cannon fire — the reason, in that account, the Kiks.ádi built in the forest at Indian River. In the 20th century Ellen Hope Lang (later Hayes), a Kiks.ádi woman and former resident of The Cottages, the Presbyterian Model Cottage Settlement, was a member of the Alaska Native Brotherhood, the craft center board and the park staff, eventually as superintendent; Thornton credits her with helping bring the cultural center to life. [3]

Climate & environment

A 2017 Park Service assessment found that the land at Sitka is rising through tectonic uplift, so relative sea level near the park is falling, about 2.29 millimeters a year by NOAA’s trend, and every park asset lies outside the projected 2050 sea-level rise zone. The hazard it singles out is tsunami: the park lies just east of the Fairweather fault system, which has tremendous potential to generate tsunamis. Across the five coastal hazards it scored, eight totem poles are the only structures rated highly vulnerable, and all four highly vulnerable transportation assets are trails, including the Totem Walk. In a separate tsunami case study, 65 percent of the park’s assets, worth over $26 million, have high tsunami vulnerability. [4]

REVISE: Exhibit panels (2 items)

Exhibit panels (2 items) 2 photos

Long before Sitka NHP became a national park, this land was home to Indigenous peoples whose connection to it spans thousands of years. The interpretive materials now being reviewed tell their story — often developed through years of formal tribal consultation, as required by federal law.

The administration has ordered revised content that includes the language: “Elite or Employee”, “Indigenous people thrived in Alaska, but Russians found it more challenging.”, “The Russian Empire, The North Pacific World”, and “Alaskan Natives named these lands first.”.

Why this matters: The Indigenous history presented at Sitka NHP was developed through formal tribal consultation — a legal requirement under federal law. The language being reviewed was often specifically requested by tribal nations whose ancestors lived on this land for thousands of years. Revising it without renewed consultation would violate both the spirit and the letter of that process.
