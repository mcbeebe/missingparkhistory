Park History

NPSHistory.com

Sitka National Historical Park in Sitka, Alaska, is the oldest federally designated park in Alaska, established as a public park in 1890 and redesignated a national monument in 1910 and a national historical park in 1972. The park preserves the site of the 1804 Battle of Sitka — the last major armed resistance by the Tlingit people against Russian colonization in Alaska. The battle occurred when Tlingit warriors defended a wooden fort (the Shís'gi Noow) against a combined Russian and Aleut force; after several days of fighting, the Tlingit withdrew in a strategic retreat. The park also houses the Southeast Alaska Indian Cultural Center, where Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian artists practice and demonstrate traditional carving, weaving, and other art forms, and maintains a renowned collection of totem poles along a temperate rainforest trail.

REVISE: Exhibit panels (2 items)

Exhibit panels (2 items) 2 photos

Long before Sitka NHP became a national park, this land was home to Indigenous peoples whose connection to it spans thousands of years. The interpretive materials now being reviewed tell their story — often developed through years of formal tribal consultation, as required by federal law.

The administration has ordered revised content that includes the language: “Elite or Employee”, “Indigenous people thrived in Alaska, but Russians found it more challenging.”, “The Russian Empire, The North Pacific World”, and “Alaskan Natives named these lands first.”.

Why this matters: The Indigenous history presented at Sitka NHP was developed through formal tribal consultation — a legal requirement under federal law. The language being reviewed was often specifically requested by tribal nations whose ancestors lived on this land for thousands of years. Revising it without renewed consultation would violate both the spirit and the letter of that process.
