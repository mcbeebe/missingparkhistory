Park History

Sourced

Glacier National Park protects 1,600 square miles of the northern Rocky Mountains in Montana, where the plains end abruptly against peaks built of sediments laid down 1.5 billion years ago and shoved, about 75 million years ago, over far younger rock along the Lewis Overthrust. From Triple Divide Peak, water runs to the Pacific, the Gulf of Mexico and Hudson Bay. Together with Waterton Lakes it is one of the few places in North America where all the native carnivores survive, and George Bird Grinnell named it the Crown of the Continent in 1908. [1] [2]

People have been drawn to this country for 10,000 years, and the park brochure calls it a landscape that has always been sacred to the Blackfeet, Salish and Kootenai peoples. Congress made Glacier the tenth national park on May 11, 1910; in 1932 it and Canada’s Waterton Lakes became the world’s first international peace park, later a biosphere reserve (1976) and World Heritage Site (1995). The park draws more than two million visitors a year; its Going-to-the-Sun Road, which crosses the Continental Divide at Logan Pass, was the first road designated a national historic civil engineering landmark. [2] [1]

The park’s namesakes are leaving. An estimated 150 glaciers existed around 1850; by 2015 only 26 remained large enough to count as active, and the 2016 foundation document describes the last remnants of alpine glaciers as disappearing quickly due to climate change. [3] [2]

Indigenous peoples

The K’tunaxa (Kootenai) and the Piikáni, one of the three Blackfeet tribes, have ties to this country reaching back well over a thousand years; the mountains are the Piikáni’s most sacred place. On January 23, 1870, in what the 2001 ethnographic overview calls the Massacre on the Marias, Col. E. M. Baker, under orders from General Sheridan, attacked a winter camp of friendly Piikáni and killed 173 or more, mostly women, children and elders. In 1883–1885, as promised rations were delayed or never delivered, 400 to 600 Piikáni died. [4]

In September 1895 three commissioners, George Bird Grinnell among them, met Blackfeet leaders to buy the mountain lands. The Piikáni asked $3 million and received $1.5 million over ten years, plus the right to hunt, fish and cut timber while the land stayed public. The strip became part of Glacier National Park in 1910, and the government came to treat those rights as lost or severely curtailed; many Blackfeet contend they never gave them up. [4]

Climate & environment

Glacier’s current ice is geologically young — formed about 7,000 years ago and at its largest around 1850, at the end of the Little Ice Age. Since 1900, the U.S. Geological Survey reports, the park region’s mean annual temperature has risen 1.33 °C, 1.8 times the global mean increase, and rain rather than snow has been the dominant form of the century’s added precipitation. Of the roughly 150 glaciers estimated in 1850, 26 were still active in 2015; Grinnell Glacier lost about 45 percent of its area between 1966 and 2015, and models project that some studied glaciers will disappear between 2030 and 2080. The loss reaches beyond scenery: glaciers act as a bank of water that keeps streams cold and flowing in late summer, and without meltwater, USGS warns, rising stream temperatures could cause local extinctions of sensitive aquatic species and harm the threatened bull trout. [3] [2]

On May 11, 1910, President William Howard Taft signed legislation creating Glacier National Park, the country's 10th national park, after conservationist George Bird Grinnell dubbed it the 'Crown of the Continent.'

Glacier National Park represents a unique high-elevation ecosystem with over 1,000 miles of trails crossing pristine wilderness. The park broke its 1 million visitor mark for the first time in 1969; by 2023 it exceeded 2.9 million annual visitors. Contains 25 major glaciers, though climate change has reduced their numbers from over 150 glaciers a century ago.

The administration has flagged for review content that includes the language: “emphasizing matters unrelated to the beauty, abundance, or grandeur”, and “emphasize matters unrelated to the beauty, abundance, or grandeur”. Officials deemed this language potentially “disparaging to certain visitors.”

The content at Glacier NP has been confirmed removed. What was once publicly accessible historical interpretation — developed over years by professional historians and park staff — has been taken down with no public record of what was changed or why.

Why this matters: Glacier exemplifies the fragility of the American wilderness—where visible climate change impacts reveal the planet's transformation. Over 2.9 million people visit this site each year.
