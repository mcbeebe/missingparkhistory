Park History

Sourced

Creole culture took root along the Cane River in 18th-century Louisiana, at what the Park Service calls the borderlands between New France and New Spain — a culture nurtured by French and Spanish colonial ways, steeped in Africanisms and enriched by American Indian contact. The park preserves two plantations, Oakland and Magnolia, 10 and 20 miles south of Natchitoches: in the Park Service’s words the most intact French Creole cotton plantations in the United States, owned and continuously operated by the same families, the Prud’hommes and the LeComte–Hertzogs, since their establishment in the 18th century. They hold 70 historic structures, including eight rare brick slave quarters at Magnolia, and nearly 500,000 museum objects and archives. [2] [1]

Jean Baptiste Lecomte obtained the land grant that grew into Magnolia in 1753. Emmanuel Prud’homme’s land on the river became the core of Bermuda, later renamed Oakland; the brochure dates his grant to 1789, a family tradition the 2019 Historic Resource Study finds no evidence for. After the cotton gin’s invention in 1793 both families turned to cotton, and between 1818 and 1821 Prud’homme’s enslaved workers built his house. After the United States took Louisiana in 1803, the 2018 brochure explains, Anglo-Americans poured in with their English language, Protestant religion and their own enslaved workers; life grew more constricted for non-whites as U.S. law removed colonial-era rights such as self-emancipation by purchase. [1] [3]

Congress created the park on November 2, 1994, in the same law that created the 116,000-acre Cane River National Heritage Area — which the 2015 foundation document calls the only instance of a park unit and a heritage area being co-legislated. The Park Service says the park tells the story of oppressive plantation labor systems founded on slavery and replaced by tenant farming, sharecropping and day labor from Reconstruction through the 1970s. [2]

Indigenous peoples

Before there was a French post at Natchitoches, this valley was Caddo country. By the end of the 1400s the Caddo numbered about 200,000, and the Red River valley was among the most densely settled parts of their homeland. Then came European disease and a years-long drought: archaeological evidence cited in the 2019 Historic Resource Study suggests the population fell by about 95 percent between 1500 and the 1680s. The Natchitoches confederacy, smallest of three, lived in villages on the river terraces between today’s Oakland Plantation and Natchitoches. [3]

In 1835 the Caddo sold all their lands by a treaty signed near Shreveport, for $30,000 in goods and horses and a promise of $50,000 over five years, pledging never to return to live as a nation within the United States. They went to Texas and later to Oklahoma, where the Caddo Nation is based today. Some stayed or returned, and the tribe still holds the Natchitoches area as its spiritual homeland. [3]

Black history

After the war a Union commander, on June 5, 1865, told freed people to stay on the premises to which they belonged, and the initial labor contracts at Bermuda paid six to ten dollars a month for men and five to eight for women. Those contracts, the park’s brochure says, differed from slavery mainly in requiring the worker’s consent; sharecropping ranged from a relatively benign feudalism to virtual bondage, the chains of slavery replaced by endless debt at the plantation store. Many stayed and rebuilt a community around church and family, often in the quarters that had housed their enslaved ancestors. [3] [1]

The first sharecropper did not leave Oakland until 1949. At Magnolia the Hertzogs housed day laborers in the brick cabins from the Depression on; electricity reached the cabins in the 1940s; mechanical pickers replaced the field crews in the 1960s, and the last residents of the cabins, the Vercher family, moved out in the early 1970s. [3] [1]

Slavery

Between 1810 and 1860 the Prud’hommes held between 50 and 145 people, placing them in the top nine percent of Louisiana slaveholders. The LeComtes held 234 people in 1860 across Magnolia and their other properties, 112 of them in the Magnolia quarters, completed in 1845. In an 1836 cotton book, Phanor Prud’homme’s overseer listed the first names of 77 people who had worked seven days a week to bring in that year’s cotton; in April 1830 a Prud’homme cousin sold Phanor eight enslaved people for just over $3,000: a couple, Thibuad and Salinette, and their six children. The Historic Resource Study cautions that much of what was recorded about these lives was written by white planters and overseers. [3]

The brochure describes the cotton year: picking began in August and ran from first light until too dark to see, the hard bolls cut pickers’ hands, and the overseer expected each adult to pick at least 200 pounds a day. [1]
