Park History

Sourced

Just over half of the small, rugged volcanic island of St. John is protected as Virgin Islands National Park: moist forest on the interior mountains, cactus scrub on the dry south-facing slopes, white beaches at Trunk, Cinnamon and Hawksnest bays, and offshore a world of mangroves, seagrass and coral. More than 800 kinds of plants grow here, among them the teyer palm, the island’s only native palm, and the bay rum tree whose leaves once scented a famous cologne; over 50 species of tropical birds breed on the island, and hawksbill and green sea turtles feed in its waters. The reefs are slow work — the fastest corals grow two or three inches a year, and large brain corals are centuries old. [1]

Congress established the park on August 2, 1956, on land that Rockefeller interests had bought and given to the federal government. In 1962 it gained 5,650 acres of submerged lands, in 1978 128 acres on Hassel Island in St. Thomas harbor, and in 2001 a presidential proclamation created the adjoining Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument on 12,708 more acres of sea floor. The Park Service now manages almost 58 percent of St. John’s land and more than 18,000 acres underwater, and in 1976 UNESCO made the park one of the first international biosphere reserves. [1] [2]

People have lived on these islands for at least 3,000 years. The park’s cultural resources include hundreds of historic structures, and its 2016 foundation document is plain about what built the plantation landscape: a plantation economy raised under Danish rule with the forced labor of enslaved people, primarily from Africa. It names the park as a place to explore the experience of the enslaved Africans and the history of slavery in the West Indies. [2]

Indigenous peoples

From St. John it is possible to sight and navigate to Puerto Rico and every island in between, to St. Croix and to all the British Virgin Islands, and the northern Virgin Islands’ cultural chronology corresponds with eastern Puerto Rico’s throughout prehistory. Kenneth Wild of Virgin Islands National Park reports that excavations at Trunk Bay and Cinnamon Bay trace a ceramic sequence beginning around AD 800, and at Cinnamon Bay a ceremonial offering place — an articulated turtle plastron, a decorated vessel of closed ark shells, a carved zemi head, shell and gold inlays for statues — preserved beneath a colonial road in use since the mid-1600s. Wild reads the change in styles around AD 1200 as a pivotal point in the development of Taíno culture. [3] The Reef Bay petroglyphs, which the 2016 foundation document counts among the best-preserved rock art in the Caribbean, were in 2016 the only one of the 22 prehistoric sites recorded on St. John listed on the National Register. The Taíno who met Columbus were decimated by European disease, enslavement and overwork under Spanish colonization. [2] [1]

Slavery

The Danes raised their flag at Coral Bay in 1718; by 1733 nearly all of St. John was in sugar cane and cotton, and enslaved people outnumbered Europeans 1,087 to 208. [1] [4] After drought, a hurricane and insects destroyed the food crops, many fled into the forest, and Governor Philip Gardelin answered with the 1733 Slave Code: flogging, amputation, public torture and execution. Early on November 23, 1733, Akwamu fighters with cane knives hidden in firewood seized the Danish fort at Coral Bay. Led by King Claes, King Juni and Kanta, they fought for six months, until about 400 French soldiers came from Martinique; many then took their own lives. The Park Service calls it the first time enslaved people took control of a colony. [5]

Slavery continued. When Annaberg’s owner James Murphy died in 1808 he held 1,300 acres and 662 enslaved people. After Britain abolished slavery across the strait in 1834, enslaved St. Johnians escaped across the narrow strait — in 1840 eleven fled together to Tortola — until Danish emancipation in 1848. Three rows of foundations from Annaberg’s enslaved laborers’ village survive west of the animal mill. [4]

Climate & environment

In September and October 2005, after record-warm seawater, an average of 90 percent of the coral cover at the long-term monitoring sites around St. John and St. Croix bleached white. Many corals began to regain their color and were then hit by disease, chiefly white plague — what National Park Service and U.S. Geological Survey scientists called a “one-two punch.” By July 2006 coral cover at the study sites had fallen 48.7 percent, the worst coral mortality the islands had seen from any cause in 40 years; it was also the first time bleaching of elkhorn coral, listed as threatened that May, had ever been observed in the Virgin Islands. [6] The park’s 2016 foundation document records that Caribbean air temperatures rose more than one degree Fahrenheit over the past century and relative sea level is climbing about 3.9 inches per 100 years, and expects more bleaching and disease outbreaks; shoreline erosion is already damaging coastal archeological sites. [2]

Entry #235 CONFIRMED REMOVED

CONFIRMED REMOVED: Interpretive materials

Interpretive materials 0 photos

On August 2, 1956, Congress established Virgin Islands National Park [NPSHistory.com] to protect tropical coral reefs, beaches, and ecosystems unique to the Caribbean.

The park protects 7,500 acres on the islands of Saint Croix and Saint John, covering both land and underwater marine ecosystems. The islands were home to Taíno and Arawak peoples for centuries before European colonization. Modern conservation efforts protect endangered sea turtles and coral reefs threatened by climate change and human activities.

From the internal review records: “The attached PDF is a mock up of a plaque that was errected at the top of the Ram Head Trail in December of 2023 in accordance with the following bill passed in the US Congress: https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-168/issue-192/house-section/article/H9682-1.”

Why this matters: The plantation ruins in this park were built and worked by enslaved Africans under Danish rule. The signs removed from Annaberg in 2026 told the stories of people like Carl Francis, born into slavery there in 1800, and of the 1733 Akwamu revolt, which the Park Service calls the first time enslaved people took control of a colony. They were developed with local historians and the community, who had worked for years to see that history told. [NPS] [NPSHistory.com] [NPS review record]

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: Publications (1 items)

Publications (1 items) 0 photos

Virgin Islands National Park in VI preserves a piece of American history that the public has trusted the National Park Service to protect and interpret. The visitor publications at this site have been developed over years by historians, subject-matter experts, and park staff — and are now being reviewed under Secretary's Order 3431.

Why this matters: Virgin Islands National Park is part of a network of over 400 national parks that serve as America's classrooms. The interpretive materials here were developed by subject-matter experts to help visitors understand the full story of this place. When historically accurate content is removed, the public loses access to its own history.
