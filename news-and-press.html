Key Background

Executive Order 14253

Signed March 27, 2025, directing the DOI to ensure park sites do not contain content that "inappropriately disparages Americans past or living, including persons living in colonial times."

Secretary's Order 3431

Issued May 20, 2025 by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, implementing the EO by requiring park staff to review all interpretive content and post feedback signs asking visitors to report "negative" information.

The Leaked Database

On March 2, 2026, a whistleblower published the internal NPS database — 879 entries of flagged signs, films, brochures, and exhibits across hundreds of parks.

Lawsuits

Three active federal lawsuits: NPCA coalition (SO 3431), City of Philadelphia (Independence Hall), and Sierra Club (FOIA). The Gilbert Baker Foundation lawsuit (Stonewall Pride flag) was settled on April 13, 2026, with the flag permanently restored.

Leaked Documents Reveal Trump’s Plan To Take Control Of Your National Parks

Wes Siler published the Interior realignment planning documents themselves (Sept. 21, 2026) — the primary source behind the reorganization reporting already in this record, readable in full for the first time. The plan would abolish the Park Service’s seven regional directors and roughly 1,100 regional management positions, replacing the regional structure with hub superintendents designated as the agency’s direction-and-accountability leadership, holding authority over budgets, staffing and every management decision at the spoke parks beneath them, whose superintendents would be left only to implement. How the agency’s 433 units divide between hubs and spokes is not specified. Siler reads the design as a route around the court order barring Secretary Doug Burgum from further reductions in force, since eliminating regional offices removes career managers without firing them and, absent a Senate-confirmed NPS director, moves superintendent selection to Burgum himself; he notes the documents’ assurance that no reductions in force are anticipated sits against their own executive summary describing the realignment as reducing duplication. The implementation timeline runs 30 days from meetings with Congress, NPS leadership and union representatives set on or around Sept. 9. Siler places the plan in the same campaign as Burgum’s signage removals and the consolidation of all public-facing communications under his office, and reports staffing down from about 20,000 to roughly 15,000. He also records that Yellowstone superintendent Cam Sholly, a 30-year Park Service veteran, announced his retirement on Sept. 11, effective at the end of the month. Similar reorganizations are indicated for BLM, the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Bureau of Reclamation.

Liz McCormick and Maria Young documented conditions at Harpers Ferry National Historical Park on Sept. 18, 2025, finding brown paper over the windows of the African American History Exhibit and a notice saying it was closed for renovation. An NPS spokesperson told the station the exhibit had been shut for more than five years because of water damage, while park staff did not answer questions about whether slavery displays elsewhere in the park had been or would be pulled. Around the corner at the John Brown Museum, placards describing the brutality of slavery were still up, as was the panel next to the 1931 Daughters of the Confederacy monument to Heyward Shepherd that challenges the “faithful slave” myth with a quote from Storer College’s Pearl Tatten. The reporters also photographed an Interior-letterhead sign near the restrooms asking visitors to report anything negative about Americans past or present. ACLU-WV executive director Eli Baumwell called the policy government-mandated historical amnesia, and NPCA’s Alan Spears said decades of work toward more accurate exhibits was being undone by people without preservation backgrounds.

Pride Month Display at NYC’s Stonewall National Monument Excludes Transgender Flags

Dave Carlin’s report establishes that the flag restriction at Stonewall National Monument began in June 2025 — roughly eight months before the February 2026 removal this record has carried as the starting point. Photographer and advocate Steven Love Menendez, who created the fence installation and won federal approval for it about nine years earlier and whose costs the National Park Service later assumed, said the agency instructed him that only the traditional rainbow flag would be displayed that year. The result was 250 rainbow flags with no transgender or progress flags, the latter carrying stripes representing communities of color. Menendez also noted that the monument’s website had reclassified him from an LGBTQ activist to an LGB activist, the same truncation applied to the site’s broader text. Visitors responded by planting unauthorized transgender flags in the soil, among them Jay Edidin of Queens; park staff said they were not cleared to comment, and the Park Service did not respond to the station. The episode is the earliest flag-policy action in this record and predates the January 2026 Interior flag guidance later cited as the rationale for the full removal.

JANM Decries Historical Erasure at Manzanar, Minidoka, and Other National Parks

The Japanese American National Museum issued a statement (June 18, 2025) denouncing the visitor-reporting signs installed at Manzanar and Minidoka National Historic Sites and at other park sites, which invite the public to flag content deemed critical of American history. The release is notable for tracing the chain of directives precisely: Executive Order 14253, then the Interior Secretary’s memorandum of May 20, 2025, then instructions issued by the National Park Service comptroller on June 9, 2025 requiring superintendents to identify and flag signs, exhibits, films and other public-facing content. That comptroller instruction is an implementation step the record had not previously captured. President and CEO Ann Burroughs said the museum is deeply disturbed by the directive at sites where Japanese Americans were unjustly incarcerated during World War II, and argued that history does not yield to censorship or political ideology but demands honest and transparent engagement. This is the earliest statement in the record from a national museum devoted to one of the communities whose park sites were directly affected.
